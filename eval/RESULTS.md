## 1. Hand-written holdout set (80 messages: 45 scam, 35 legitimate)

Written by the author from public RBI, NPCI, I4C and DoT advisories and committed before any rule existed (see git history). The same person then wrote the rules with these scam types in mind, so a perfect score here shows the rules cover the advisory patterns they were built for. It is not an unbiased estimate of real-world accuracy. The Classifier row shows the classifier deciding alone; in Kedayam as shipped it is only a hint and does not change the result.

## 2. Public dataset test split (876 messages)

Mishra and Soni (2022) SMS Phishing Dataset, CC BY 4.0, deduplicated, 15% stratified test split with seed 42. Positive class is spam or smishing. The classifier was trained on the 70% train split and its threshold (0.5) was chosen on the 15% validation split. The rules were not written from this dataset; most of its spam is marketing spam (ringtones, prize texts), which Kedayam's rules are not designed to catch.

| System | Precision | Recall (flagged) | Recall (Warning only) | False alarm rate | TP | FP | FN | TN |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Rules only | 1.000 | 0.333 | 0.020 | 0.000 | 50 | 0 | 100 | 726 |
| Classifier only | 0.973 | 0.967 | 0.000 | 0.006 | 145 | 4 | 5 | 722 |
| Kedayam as shipped (rules decide) | 1.000 | 0.333 | 0.020 | 0.000 | 50 | 0 | 100 | 726 |

Rules recall by original label: smishing 49 / 92, marketing spam 1 / 58.

The classifier scores well here because this split comes from the same source as its training data. On the hand-written Indian set it raised false alarms on ordinary bank and service messages, which is why it does not decide the result. See eval/HISTORY.md.
